## Q1. How common are the problems in US women about 58–75? (prevalence)

### Takeaway
All three problems are common in this group. About 29% of US women aged 65+ report a fall each year (BRFSS 2018 and 2020). 27% of women aged 65+ have osteoporosis, and more than half of women aged 50+ have low bone mass (NHANES 2017–2018). About 46% of adults aged 65+ had back pain in the last 3 months (NHIS 2019). Hyperkyphosis affects roughly 20–40% of older adults. Good US data are thin for three items: self-reported "balance problems", fear of falling, and chair-rise difficulty specifically in women aged 60–74.

### Cited Findings
**Falls (niche 1)**
- 2018 BRFSS: 27.5% of US adults aged 65+ reported at least one fall in the past year and 10.2% a fall injury. Women were higher than men: 29.1% fell and 11.9% had a fall injury. (Moreland et al., MMWR 2020;69(27)) — [MMWR 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6927a5.htm); [PMC7732363](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7732363/) (snippet)
- 2020 BRFSS: 14 million adults aged 65+ (27.6%) reported falling in the previous year. Women 28.9%, men 26.1%. By state the range was 19.9% (Illinois) to 38.0% (Alaska). In 2021, 38,742 adults aged 65+ died from unintentional falls (78.0 per 100,000). (Kakara et al., MMWR 2023;72(35)) — [MMWR 2023](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7235a1.htm) (snippet)
- 2020 fall death rate for ages 65+: 65.6 per 100,000 (men 70.3, women 61.8). Rates rose from 2000 to 2020 in every age band, including 65–74. — [NCHS Data Brief 449 (2022)](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/121836) (snippet)

**Balance problems / dizziness (niche 1)**
- 2008 NHIS Balance supplement: 14.84% of US adults reported dizziness or balance problems in the past 12 months. 9.07% felt "off-balance or unsteady", 2.94% had difficulty walking in the dark and 2.38% on uneven surfaces. Of those affected, 63.9% were female and 26.0% were over 65. — [Balance Disorder Trends in US Adults 2008–2016, OTO Open 2023 (PMC10242407)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10242407/) (snippet; the figures are 2008)

**Fear of falling (niche 1)**
- NHATS 2011–2015 sample of nearly 2,000 community-dwelling adults aged 65+: 21% reported fear of falling. — [Bidirectional relationship between subjective age and fear of falling, NHATS (PMC7740936)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7740936/) (snippet)
- NHATS Round 7 (5,559 adults aged 65+): fear of falling was reported by 48.8% of those who fell in the past year and 46.8% of those who fell in the past month. — [Frontiers in Public Health 2021 (PMC8257838)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8257838/) (snippet)
- Internationally, 20.8%–57% of non-institutionalized people aged 62+ report fear of falling. Activity restriction from that fear is more likely in women, older people and people who have fallen. — [Archives of Public Health review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/0778-7367-70-1) (snippet; not US-specific)

**Difficulty rising from a chair (niche 1)**
- Difficulty rising from a chair affects "up to 2 million (8%)" of noninstitutionalized people aged 65+. This is a 1990s US estimate. — [J Am Geriatr Soc 1996/1997 (UMich Deep Blue)](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/111137/1/j.1532-5415.1996.tb06402.x.pdf) (snippet; old data)
- Another snippet, from a source that could not be checked: the "risk of being unable to rise out of a low chair without difficulty" was 14% for women aged 50–69 and 25% for women aged 70–74. — [Health and Quality of Life Outcomes 2010 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/b959aa9952944aaa8542815a32fdc0bf) (snippet; country and method unverified)
- SHARE (Europe, ages 50+): 18.6% reported difficulty rising from a chair. — same search result set (snippet; not US)

**Osteoporosis / low bone mass (niche 2)**
- NHANES 2017–2018, ages 50+, femoral neck and/or lumbar spine (Sarafrazi et al., NCHS Data Brief 405, 2021):
  - Osteoporosis, age-adjusted: 12.6% overall, **19.6% of women** vs 4.4% of men.
  - Low bone mass: **51.5% of women** vs 33.5% of men.
  - **Women aged 65+: 27.1% have osteoporosis.** Low bone mass is **50.3% in women aged 50–64** and **52.9% in women aged 65+**.
  - Age-adjusted osteoporosis in women rose from 14.0% (2007–2008) to 19.6% (2017–2018). Low bone mass showed no significant trend.
  — [NCHS Data Brief 405](https://cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db405.htm) (snippets from two searches)

**Back pain and kyphosis (niche 3)**
- NHIS 2019 (Lucas et al., NCHS Data Brief 415, 2021): 39.0% of US adults had back pain in the past 3 months. This rose with age to **45.6% at ages 65+**. Women 40.6% vs men 37.2%. — [NCHS Data Brief 415](https://cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db415.htm) (snippet)
- Reported prevalence and incidence of hyperkyphosis in older adults range from about 20% to 40%. Kyphosis rises faster in women, especially around menopause. (Kado et al., narrative review, Ann Intern Med 2007;147:330–338, cited in later reviews.) — [Frontiers in Endocrinology 2020 mini-review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fendo.2020.00005/full); [PMC2907357](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2907357) (snippet)

### Inferences
- Falls are the best-measured problem: about 1 in 3.5 women aged 65+ report a fall each year. For women 58–64 the rate is probably lower, but no age- and sex-specific US figure was retrieved.
- Bone: about 1 in 2 women aged 50–64 have low bone mass, and about 1 in 4 women aged 65+ have osteoporosis. This is a very large pool, but most of these women have no symptoms. They feel it as a diagnosis (a DXA result), not as a daily problem, which differs from "I feel wobbly" or "my back aches".
- Back pain is the most common of the three as a felt problem (about 46% of 65+ over 3 months). "Posture" or kyphosis lacks a US survey figure. 20–40% is a review-level range, not NHANES or NHIS.
- The data support a "steadiness + strong legs" niche (falls, fear of falling, chair rise) as a shared root for all three. The WFG and SSS guidelines both put strength + balance first, including for osteoporosis.

### Gaps
- No retrieved US figure for **self-reported balance problems, chair-rise difficulty or fear of falling specifically in women aged 60–74**. NHIS 2008 is the latest balance supplement found. The 2016 NHIS update and PMC4839981 tables could not be opened.
- Osteoporosis prevalence for **women aged 50–64** (NHANES 2017–2018) was not shown in the snippets. Do not use a number without checking Data Brief 405 directly.
- No NHANES/NHIS prevalence for **kyphosis** and no US figure for the **number of falls per person per year** in women 60–74. BRFSS reports "≥1 fall" and "≥2 falls" percentages; the ≥2 figure was not retrieved.

## Q2. Niche 1 — steadiness, balance and sit-to-stand: guidelines, effect sizes, norms

### Takeaway
This niche has the strongest evidence of the three. Exercise lowers the rate of falls in community-dwelling older people by about 23%, and balance/functional exercise by about 24% (Cochrane 2019, high certainty). The World Falls Guidelines 2022 recommend balance + functional exercise at least 3 times a week, progressed, for at least 12 weeks. Home strength programmes improve the 30-second chair stand by roughly 2–6 reps over 8–12 weeks. The Rikli & Jones norms give an easy, valid home benchmark.

### Cited Findings
**Guidelines**
- World Guidelines for Falls Prevention (Montero-Odasso et al., Age and Ageing 2022;51(9):afac205). Programmes for community-dwelling older people should include **balance-challenging and functional exercises (e.g. sit-to-stand, stepping)**, offered **≥3 times a week**, "individualised, progressed in intensity for at least 12 weeks and continued longer for greater effect". — [BGS summary](https://bgs.org.uk/node/6848); [physioupdate](https://physioupdate.co.uk/?p=1462) (snippet). The repo note records grade 1A for balance + functional exercise and 1B for tai chi and/or resistance training. It also records that walking alone is unlikely to reduce falls. — [Age and Ageing 2022, PMC9523684](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9523684/) (full text read 06/10, repo note)

**Meta-analyses**
- Sherrington et al., Cochrane 2019 (abridged in BJSM 2020): exercise reduces the **rate of falls by 23%** (RaR 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83; 59 trials, 12,981 participants; high certainty). **Balance and functional exercise**: RaR 0.76 (0.70–0.81), a 24% reduction (39 trials, 7,920 participants; high certainty). — [Cochrane news](https://www.cochrane.org/news/new-cochrane-review-assesses-benefits-and-harms-exercise-preventing-falls-older-people-living); [BJSM 2020;54:885](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/15/885) (snippet)
- Otago Exercise Programme meta-analysis (Thomas et al., Age and Ageing 2010): 7 trials, 1,503 participants, **mean age 81.6**. Fall rate IRR 0.68 (95% CI 0.56–0.79), about a 32% reduction. Deaths over 12 months RR 0.45 (0.25–0.80). The commonly quoted "35%" figure is close to but not the same as this pooled IRR. Otago is home-based but delivered by a physiotherapist with home visits. — [Age and Ageing 2010;39(6):681](https://academic.oup.com/ageing/article/39/6/681/9467) (snippet); a 2025 Otago meta-analysis also exists — [Frontiers Public Health 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1522952/full) (not read)

**Norms: 30-second chair stand (Rikli & Jones 1999 norms, used by CDC STEADI)**
| Age (women) | Below average | Average range | Above average |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60–64 | < 12 | 12–17 | > 17 |
| 65–69 | < 11 | 11–16 | > 16 |
| 70–74 | < 10 | 10–15 | > 15 |
| 75–79 | < 10 | (10–15, per STEADI snippet) | — |
- The first three rows are from the [bpac NZ STEADI-based sheet](https://bpac.org.nz/falls/docs/The_30-Second_Chair_Stand_Test.pdf) and [topendsports](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/chair-stand.htm) (snippets). CDC STEADI says "a below average score indicates a risk for falls" and gives women 75–79 < 10. The test uses a chair with a straight back, no arm rests, seat 17" high. — [Oregon STEADI sheet](https://oregon.gov/oha/ph/PreventionWellness/SafeLiving/FallPrevention/Documents/ChairStandTest.pdf); [NJ STEADI PDF](https://www.nj.gov/humanservices/doas/forms/STEADI-Assessment-30Sec-508.pdf) (snippet; the repo note S17 confirms the chair spec)

**Change in chair-stand performance with training (effect size and weeks)**
- 12-week chair-based, low-load elastic-band resistance training, 168 older women (mean age 75.7), 2 × 12–15 reps twice a week: significant improvement in the 30-s chair stand (F = 12.78, p < 0.05). — [search result set](https://www.epistemonikos.org/en/documents/b7973e06df819e555f05f287f1126bc3d331d8b2) (snippet; size of change not given)
- 12-week body-weight resistance training, 23 community-dwelling Japanese women, twice a week: CS-30 rose from 19.8 ± 3.8 to 25.5 ± 5.6 (about +5.7 reps). — [citedrive record](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/effect-of-body-weight-based-resistance-training-on-balance-ability-and-fear-of-falling-in-community-dwelling-older-japanese-women) (snippet)
- 8-week programme, 26 older women: the experimental group went from 18.3 to 26.6 reps, **but the control group also rose from 17.9 to 23.7**. Learning effects and repeat testing inflate chair-stand gains. — [AAHPERD 2011 abstract](https://aahperd.confex.com/aahperd/2011/webprogram/Paper15976.html) (snippet)
- Self-guided online programme for midlife and older women: CST +22.8% (+2.5 reps on average) after one 18-session series and +41.5% after four series. **This comes from an industry press release that features the vendor (Osteoboost), not a peer-reviewed RCT.** — [MedTech Innovator](https://medtechinnovator.org/new-study-shows-self-guided-online-exercise-program-significantly-improves-strength-and-reduces-fall-risk-in-midlife-and-older-women-featuring-osteoboost-health/) (snippet)

### Inferences
- A realistic, honest in-app expectation is "+2 to +5 chair stands in 30 s over 8–12 weeks" for a deconditioned woman. Many 58–75 newcomers will start in the 10–15 "average" band. Part of the early gain is learning or familiarisation, so show trends rather than celebrating a first-week jump.
- The falls-reduction effect (−23% / −24%) is a population-level result of supervised or structured programmes over months. It supports the *design* of the programme, not a promise to an individual user (see Q7).

### Gaps
- No pooled meta-analytic effect size (SMD) for **home, unsupervised strength training on the 30-s chair stand** in women aged 58–75 was retrieved.
- Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the 30-s chair stand in this group: not retrieved. Reported values of about 2 reps exist in the literature but were not verified this session.

## Q3. Niche 2 — bone health / osteoporosis-safe exercise

### Takeaway
Exercise has a real but **small** effect on bone density: an SMD of about 0.33–0.40 in postmenopausal women over at least 6 months. The bigger BMD gains (LIFTMOR, about +3% lumbar spine vs −1% in controls over 8 months) came from **supervised, high-intensity** lifting, which a voice-led beginner home app cannot replicate. Fracture reduction is about twice as strong in supervised trials, and non-supervised trials were not statistically significant. Guidelines (Strong, Steady and Straight 2022) ask for resistance + impact, strength + balance, and spinal extension, and stress "how to move safely" rather than banning exercises.

### Cited Findings
- Kemmler et al., Frontiers in Physiology 2020 meta-analysis: 75 studies, 5,300 postmenopausal women, interventions ≥ 6 months. Exercise effect on BMD: **SMD 0.37 (0.25–0.50) lumbar spine, 0.33 (0.23–0.43) femoral neck, 0.40 (0.28–0.51) total hip**. The authors called this a "significant but rather low effect". — [Frontiers Physiol 2020;11:652](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.00652/pdf) (snippet)
- LIFTMOR (Watson et al., JBMR 2018;33(2):211). Postmenopausal women with low bone mass (T < −1.0) did **8 months** of twice-weekly, 30-minute, **supervised** high-intensity resistance + impact training (5 × 5 at > 85% 1RM). The control group did a **home-based low-intensity** programme. Lumbar spine BMD: **+2.9 ± 2.8% vs −1.2 ± 2.8%** (p < 0.001). — [JBMR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709) (snippet)
- Hoffmann/Kemmler et al., 2022 meta-analyses on fractures (FAU repository): exercise reduces low-trauma overall and major osteoporotic fractures in middle-aged to older adults (one analysis: 11 studies, about 9,700 participant-years per arm). **Supervision mattered**:
  - Predominantly supervised trials: IR 0.44 (0.27–0.73) overall and 0.38 (0.19–0.76) major fractures.
  - Predominantly non-supervised trials: IR 0.83 (0.60–1.14) and 0.82 (0.64–1.05), not significant.
  — [open.fau.de](https://open.fau.de/items/41cf74c6-0574-4750-86de-5b5a3eb4b87b/full); [cris.fau.de](https://cris.fau.de/publications/286198176?lang=de_DE) (snippet; journal named in the snippet as Osteoporos Int / JBMR, not verified)
- Strong, Steady and Straight UK consensus (Brooke-Wavell, Skelton et al., BJSM 2022;56:837–846):
  - Resistance + impact exercise to maximise bone strength.
  - Strength + balance to reduce falls.
  - **Spinal extension exercise** to improve posture and possibly reduce falls and vertebral fractures.
  - People with vertebral fracture or multiple low-trauma fractures should usually keep impact up to the level of brisk walking.
  - Avoid postures with a high degree of spinal flexion.
  — [PubMed 35577538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35577538/) (snippet). The repo note adds resistance 2–3 days a week, strength + balance ≥ 2 times a week, and a "how to, not don't do" tone. — [PMC9304091](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9304091) (full text read 06/10, repo note)
- International Osteoporosis Foundation: avoid forward spinal flexion, especially loaded, and forceful twisting; favour trunk extension, strengthening and balance. — repo note S32, [IOF page](https://www.osteoporosis.foundation/health-professionals/prevention/exercise/exercise-individuals-with-osteoporosis) (read by earlier pass)

### Inferences
- An app **cannot claim** "builds bone" or "improves bone density". The effect is small, needs ≥ 6–8 months, needs DXA to measure, and the strongest data come from supervised high-load protocols. The safe positioning is "osteoporosis-aware" programming (no loaded flexion, extension work, strength + balance) as a design quality. Do not present it as an outcome.
- Bone outcomes **cannot be self-measured with a phone**. Only proxies (chair stand, balance holds, back-extensor endurance) are measurable, so a "bone" niche has a weak visible-progress loop for the user.
- The bone niche shares exercises with niches 1 and 3 (strength, balance, extension). A "bone-safe" filter is a natural sub-feature rather than a standalone niche.

### Gaps
- No RCT retrieved of an **app- or video-delivered** bone-loading programme with BMD outcomes in women 58–75.
- Adherence and dropout figures for LIFTMOR and the Kemmler meta-analysis were not in the snippets.
- Exact dose wording in SSS (e.g. impact reps per day) was not re-verified this session.

## Q4. Niche 3 — posture / thoracic kyphosis / back extensors / low back discomfort

### Takeaway
Back-extensor and posture training reduces kyphosis modestly. SHEAF found a −3° Cobb angle between-group difference after 6 months of PT-led group classes, 3 times a week. Pooled SMD across trials is large (−1.4) but based on few, small studies. A small 1990s–2002 RCT (Sinaki) reported fewer vertebral fractures 8 years after 2 years of back-extensor training. For chronic low back pain, exercise reduces pain versus no treatment (Cochrane 2021, moderate certainty), but the effect on function and versus other care is small.

### Cited Findings
- **SHEAF RCT** (Katzman et al., Osteoporos Int 2017; PMID 28689306): 99 participants (71 women), mean age 70.6, baseline Cobb angle 57.4°. The intervention was group spine-strengthening + posture training led by a physical therapist, **1 h, 3 times a week, for 6 months**; controls got 4 health-education meetings. Result: **−3.0° between-group difference in Cobb angle**, about the size of change caused by an incident vertebral fracture. Self-image and satisfaction with appearance also improved. — [ClinicalTrials NCT01751685](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01751685); [MDedge summary](https://www.mdedge9-ma1.mdedge.com/rheumatology/clinical-edge/summary/osteoporosis/exercise-and-posture-training-reduced-kyphosis) (snippet)
- González-Gálvez et al., PLoS One 2019 meta-analysis: 10 RCTs. 7 measured kyphosis angle, with a **large effect of exercise (SMD −1.40)**. **Strengthening appears more relevant than stretching**. — [PLoS One 2019 (PMC6488071)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6488071/) (snippet). An earlier systematic review: [Exercise for improving age-related hyperkyphotic posture, PubMed 23850611](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23850611/) (not read)
- **Sinaki et al., Bone 2002;30(6):836–841**: 50 healthy postmenopausal women **aged 58–75**. 27 did progressive prone back-extensor strengthening for 2 years and 23 were controls, with follow-up at 10 years.
  - Back-extensor strength, exercise group: 39.4 kg at baseline, 66.8 kg at 2 years, 32.9 kg at 10 years.
  - Back-extensor strength, controls: 36.9, 49.0 and 26.9 kg (significant difference at 10 years).
  - A secondary source gives vertebral compression fractures of about 1% (exercise) vs 4% (control). The often-quoted "1.6% vs 4.3%" is understood to be per vertebral bodies examined; this exact figure was **not verified from the abstract this session**.
  — [clinician.com summary](https://www.clinician.com/articles/80037-back-strengthening-can-help-reduce-fractures); [HealthDay](https://consumer.healthday.com/bone-and-joint-information-4/bone-joint-and-tendon-news-72/keeping-your-backbone-strong-507939.html) (snippet). Small, old and non-blinded, but its age band (58–75) matches the target almost exactly.
- **Chronic low back pain** (Hayden et al., Cochrane 2021, CD009790.pub2: 249 trials, 24,486 participants): exercise **probably reduces pain** vs no treatment, usual care or placebo (moderate certainty). Effects on **functional limitations** vs no treatment, and on pain/function vs **other conservative treatments**, are probably small. The review used 15/100 as the MCID for pain. — [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009790.pub2/information); [PEDro summary](https://pedro.org.au/english/systematic-review-found-that-exercise-reduces-pain-more-than-no-treatment-in-adults-with-chronic-non-specific-low-back-pain) (snippet)
- The Cochrane 2023 network meta-analysis found all exercise types better than minimal treatment, with Pilates, McKenzie and functional restoration ranking highest for pain and function. — [Cochrane CD015608](https://cochranelibrary.com/es/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD015608/information/es) (snippet; not specific to older adults)

### Inferences
- "Stand taller / posture" has a plausible evidence base (extension strengthening), but the measured effects are **small (a few degrees)**, come from **supervised, 3×/week, 6-month** programmes, and include mostly women around 70. The improvement in body image (SHEAF) may be the more user-felt benefit.
- Back pain is common, but making it a niche invites medical-claim risk ("relieves back pain") and a population with red-flag causes. It fits better as a comfort and mobility framing ("move more comfortably") than as a promise.
- A phone **cannot validly measure** Cobb angle or kyphosis. Proxies (wall-to-tragus or "occiput-to-wall", timed prone/standing back-extensor hold) are low-tech and need a helper or a mirror, which makes this niche's self-tracking weak.

### Gaps
- Hayden 2021 pooled mean differences (points out of 100) were not shown in the snippets. Do not cite a number.
- No **home/app-delivered** kyphosis RCT and no adherence figures for SHEAF were retrieved.
- No validated smartphone posture or kyphosis measure was found.

## Q5. Home / unsupervised vs supervised; app- and video-delivered programmes; adherence

### Takeaway
Unsupervised programmes still work, but less well. Supervised balance and strength training beats unsupervised training by an SMD of about 0.3–0.5. Supervised trials also show about twice the fracture reduction. The best app-delivered evidence is **StandingTall** (Delbaere, BMJ 2021): 16% fewer falls and 20% fewer injurious falls over 2 years in people aged 70+, with 52% still exercising at 2 years. Typical home-exercise adherence is poor: a pooled estimate of only about 21% "fully adherent", and 14–35% dropout.

### Cited Findings
- **Lacroix et al., Sports Med 2017** (supervised vs unsupervised balance/resistance training, older adults): supervised training was superior for
  - static steady-state balance (SMDbs 0.28, n.s.)
  - dynamic steady-state balance (0.35, p = 0.02)
  - proactive balance (0.24, p = 0.05)
  - balance test batteries (0.53, p = 0.02)
  - **muscle strength/power (0.51, p = 0.04)**
  The largest benefit came from 10–29 extra supervised sessions (strength/power SMD 1.12). — [PubMed 28573401](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28573401/) (snippet)
- **StandingTall RCT** (Delbaere et al., BMJ 2021;373:n740): 503 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 70, independent, without cognitive impairment. The intervention target was **2 h/week** of a home-based, app-delivered balance programme, vs control, for **2 years**. Results:
  - Falls rate **16% lower**; injurious falls rate **20% lower**.
  - Adherence: **80% still using at 6 months, 68% at 12 months, 52% at 24 months**.
  — [BMJ 2021](https://www.bmj.com/content/373/bmj.n740); [NeuRA media release](https://neura.edu.au/news-media/media-releases/standingtall-a-new-e-health-exercise-program-helps-prevent-falls-in-older-people-by-up-to-20-per-cent) (snippet; CIs, primary-outcome nuance and exact minutes/week not verified)
- **Home-exercise adherence** (systematic review of home exercise for fall prevention in older adults):
  - Pooled "fully adherent": **21% (95% CI 15–29%; range 0–68%)**.
  - Completion 65–86%, i.e. 14–35% dropout.
  - Sessions attended: 58–77% of those available; 1.5–3 home sessions a week.
  - Better adherence with balance or walking content, moderate home-visit support, physiotherapist-led delivery and no flexibility training.
  - Adherence level was **not** associated with efficacy.
  — [Epistemonikos record](https://epistemonikos.org/en/documents/2a2f2956a64f055ddfff3cf451b7f21a7f08d092) (snippet; review authorship/year not verified, possibly Simek et al., Prev Med 2012)
- Fracture meta-analysis: non-supervised exercise trials showed non-significant fracture reductions (IR about 0.82–0.83) vs significant reductions in supervised trials (IR 0.38–0.44). — [open.fau.de](https://open.fau.de/items/41cf74c6-0574-4750-86de-5b5a3eb4b87b/full) (snippet)

### Inferences
- An app is in the "unsupervised" arm by definition. The honest expectation is smaller effects than the headline trials. StandingTall is the best proof that an app-only balance programme can move outcomes in older adults, but its participants were 70+ Australians in a research context with 2 h/week targets.
- Retention is the main risk to impact. StandingTall's 52% at 2 years is exceptional. Typical home programmes see about 1 in 5 fully adherent, so design should target "regular partial adherence".

### Gaps
- No retrieved RCT of an app or video programme in **US women aged 58–75 specifically**, and none for bone or posture outcomes.
- StandingTall 12-month falls result, confidence intervals and median weekly minutes: not verified (BMJ blocked).

## Q6. What can a phone measure validly at home?

### Takeaway
Feasible and reasonably valid:
- **Counting sit-to-stands** with the phone IMU: correlation about 0.89 and ICC about 0.97 vs a human counter in a small iPhone pilot.
- **Step counts** on the iPhone: about 2–4% error at normal pace, but undercounting up to about 9% at slow pace.
- Apple's own **Walking Steadiness / Mobility metrics** in HealthKit, derived from carrying the phone while walking.

**Static balance via the phone accelerometer** correlates only moderately with a force plate (r about 0.5) and is less reliable. A simple **timed hold** (seconds in tandem or single-leg stance) is the more robust measure.

### Cited Findings
- iPhone 30-s chair stand pilot (JMIR mHealth uHealth 2017;5(10):e166): 24 participants. Accelerometer-based counts vs a manual rater: Pearson r = 0.890; ICC across trials = 0.968. — [PMC5681723](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5681723/) (snippet; small sample)
- A self-administered home 30-s chair stand system (low-cost sensor + Android app guiding older adults) counted transitions in perfect agreement with manual counts (r = 1) in its study. Another mHealth app counts reps with the phone in a chest strap. — [Sensors 2020 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/85e2c784b52145958ca3737d770978b6); [UPM portal](https://portalcientifico.upm.es/en/ipublic/item/9065987) (snippet). Relevant to the app's "Held to my chest" motion option.
- Smartphone IMU vs force plate for postural sway, 97 adults aged 50–90 (eyes open, eyes closed, semi-tandem, dual-task): **robust correlations of about 0.5** (age-adjusted). Test-retest reliability was lower than the force plate, though both detected condition differences with similar Cohen's d. — [bioRxiv 2020](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.05.29.123620.full.pdf) (snippet; preprint)
- iPhone Health app step count: MAPE < 4% walking straight or zigzag (2.37% level, 3.78% uphill). For slower walking on a treadmill the iPhone **underestimated steps by up to 9.4%**. — [Acta IMEKO](https://acta.imeko.org/index.php/acta-imeko/article/view/1673); [9to5mac/Gigazine summary](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20171208-iphone-miss-your-step) (snippet)
- Apple **Walking Steadiness** (iOS 15+): estimates balance, stability and coordination from iPhone motion sensors while walking with the phone carried, with classes **OK / Low / Very Low**. It was developed with data from the Apple Heart and Movement Study (> 100,000 participants). Mobility metrics (walking speed, step length, double support, asymmetry) come from iPhone 8 and later, iOS 14+. — [Apple white paper, May 2021](https://Www.apple.com/uk/healthcare/docs/site/Measuring_Walking_Quality_Through_iPhone_Mobility_Metrics.pdf); [STAT News 2021](https://www.statnews.com/2021/07/15/apple-walking-steadiness-research/) (snippet)
- A peer-reviewed study (2023) assessed concurrent validity and test-retest reliability of Health-app gait parameters during a 6-minute walk in children, adults and seniors (28 seniors) against inertial sensors. — [search result set](https://primarycarenotebook.com/pages/neurology/balance-and-gait-algorithms-on-a-smartphone) (snippet; the result values were not shown)
- Self-administered phone-based balance and gait assessments: walking-variable models classified fallers with AUC 0.82 (lab) and 0.89 (home), rising to 0.92 with feet-together sway path length. — [BGS abstract](https://www.bgs.org.uk/faller-classification-ability-of-self-administered-balance-and-gait-assessments-conducted-using-a) (snippet)

### Inferences
- The most defensible in-app self-measures are:
  1. **30-s chair stand count**, either tapped or counted by the user, or auto-counted with the phone held to the chest. Compare it only to the user's own previous score; Rikli/STEADI bands are optional context.
  2. **Timed balance holds** (side-by-side → semi-tandem → tandem → single-leg, up to 10/30 s), recorded as seconds held.
  3. **Steps / walking minutes** from HealthKit.
  4. Reading HealthKit **Walking Steadiness / walking speed** if the user permits, without re-deriving fall risk.
- Raw-accelerometer sway scores are research-grade only and should not be presented as a "balance score" (accuracy can't be validated; see Q7).
- Bone and kyphosis have no valid phone measure.

### Gaps
- Peer-reviewed accuracy statistics for Apple Walking Steadiness (sensitivity, specificity, AUC for 12-month falls) were not retrievable (apple.com blocked). Treat it as Apple's own published method.
- No validation found of a **phone-on-chair or phone-on-table** (not body-worn) chair-stand counting method.

## Q7. Claim limits — what can be said without medical claims (App Store 1.4.1)

### Takeaway
Guideline 1.4.1 puts apps giving health information or measurements under greater scrutiny and requires validated methodology for accuracy claims. Safe wording describes **what the user does and feels**, for example:
- "exercises for steadier balance and stronger legs"
- "practice getting up from a chair"
- "osteoporosis-aware moves"
- "stand-tall exercises"

Outcome or disease claims are unsafe, for example:
- "prevents falls" or "reduces fall risk by 23%"
- "builds bone density" or "reverses osteoporosis"
- "fixes kyphosis" or "relieves back pain"

The evidence for those outcome claims comes from supervised trials and population averages.

### Cited Findings
- App Store Review Guideline 1.4.1:
  - "Medical apps that could provide inaccurate data or information, or that could be used for diagnosing or treating patients may be reviewed with greater scrutiny."
  - Apps must "clearly disclose data and methodology to support accuracy claims relating to health measurements"; if accuracy cannot be validated, the app will be rejected.
  - Apps should remind users to check with a doctor before medical decisions.
  — [Apple developer forum thread on 1.4.1](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/807508); [ConductAtlas record of 1.4.1](https://conductatlas.com/platform/apple/apple-app-store-review-guidelines/provision/CA-P-018796/medical-apps-with-inaccurate-data-may-be-rejected/) (snippet)
- Evidence anchors that show *why* outcome claims overreach:
  - Falls reduction is a population rate ratio from structured programmes ([Cochrane 2019](https://www.cochrane.org/news/new-cochrane-review-assesses-benefits-and-harms-exercise-preventing-falls-older-people-living)).
  - Unsupervised training is weaker ([Lacroix 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28573401/)).
  - Non-supervised exercise did not significantly reduce fractures ([FAU meta-analysis](https://open.fau.de/items/41cf74c6-0574-4750-86de-5b5a3eb4b87b/full)).
  - BMD effects are small ([Kemmler 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.00652/pdf)).
  - Kyphosis change is about 3° under PT supervision ([SHEAF](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01751685)).
- The repo already bans "prevents falls", "reduces fall risk", "improves balance by X%", "strengthens bones", "reverses osteoporosis", "fixes posture" and similar phrases. — `docs/research/2026-09-30-exercise-standards.md` lines ~505–506 (repo)

### Inferences
- **Steadiness niche:** "Feel steadier on your feet", "stronger legs for getting up from a chair", and "track your chair-stand count over time" (the user's own number) describe activity and self-observed function. "Fall prevention", "lower your fall risk" and showing a STEADI "fall risk" label from a self-test cross into risk assessment and claims. If the 30-s chair stand is offered, frame it as "your leg-strength check", compared with the user's own past results. Avoid labelling "below average = fall risk" unless accuracy and methodology are disclosed. That wording is CDC's clinical-screening language.
- **Bone niche:** "Bone-friendly" or "osteoporosis-aware moves (no deep forward bends)" is a design statement. "Builds bone" or "improves bone density" is an outcome claim the evidence does not support for unsupervised low-load programmes.
- **Posture/back niche:** "Stand-tall exercises", "back-strengthening moves" and "move more comfortably" are acceptable. "Corrects kyphosis" and "treats back pain" are medical claims. Back pain also needs red-flag screening copy ("check with your doctor if…").
- Citing guidelines (WFG 2022, SSS 2022) as the *basis for the programme design* is safer than quoting their effect sizes as user outcomes. Per the repo rule, use source names only in a credits line with "not affiliated with or endorsed by".

### Gaps
- No Apple precedent found (rejection or approval) specific to the wording "fall prevention" in a consumer exercise app. The line above is an inference from the 1.4.1 text plus the repo's existing copy rules.
- No guidance retrieved on whether showing CDC STEADI norm bands inside an app triggers the 1.4.1 "accuracy claims" requirement.
